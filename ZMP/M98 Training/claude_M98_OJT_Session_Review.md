## 1. DDA Transit — the escalating one

**Critical.** Six of seven sessions, one repeating shape: an aircraft ends up inside the Departure Dispersal Area without the coordination that is supposed to keep it clear of MSP departures.

- **Session 1** — N172ER off FCM 10R, left on runway heading, trucks straight through the dispersal area with no point-out.
- **Session 3** — N127GA, same shape, this time on a bravo transition.
- **Session 5** — N442PL, another bravo transition, turns into the DDA at nearly the worst possible moment against a 30R departure. No violation — but only because of timing, not planning.
- **Session 6** — the near-misses stop being near. A departing SCHEP and an arriving TORGY lose separation inside the 30/17 dispersal area, the TORGY descended to 4,000 while still over FCM.
- **Session 7** — N172ER again. Same airport, same destination (EAU), same shape as session 1, four sessions later. The correction changes tone: not "fix it when you see it," but "pick a better coordination window and vector around it before the DDA is even part of the conversation."

This is the priority — not because it is the hardest single skill, but because it is the one place "I'll catch it if it happens" has already failed once.

**Documented Requirement — MSP-M98 LOA §5(a)(3)–(4), §8(i)(2), §8(k):** The DDA runs surface–7,000 ft in the area itself, 3,500–7,000 ft over the FCM/MIC satellite cutouts, and 4,000–7,000 ft in DDA extensions. Runway 22 departures get a DDA heading plus an initial 3,000 ft, climbed per the altitude table only once clear of the DDA. Point-outs are pre-approved (APO) for DDA transits on the MIC RNAV 32 and STP RNAV/ILS approaches — anything outside those specific conditions needs a manual point-out to MSP tower.
> *Confidence:* sub-paragraph lettering as retrieved from the LOA text — worth a quick cross-check against the trainee's own copy.

**Why it happens:** The DDA sits inside airspace tower is actively using to launch departures. From the scope it can look like open space between satellite traffic and the en route structure. From tower's side it is a runway extension. Every aircraft that clips it is a potential conflict with whatever tower just launched — and tower cannot see the satellite traffic coming unless told.

**The earlier moment:** Every one of these gets corrected after the aircraft is already near or inside the DDA. The earlier moment is before the turn or climb: does this track clip the dispersal footprint, and if it does, has tower already been told — or can it be routed around so the question never comes up?

**Drill situation:** 30/17 configuration. A SCHEP departure is rolling and climbing out. Also vectoring a TORGY arrival for the 30L final — level at 6,000, about to start down, track running right over the FCM area. The dispersal area in this configuration is large.
**Ask:** What do you do with the TORGY's descent right now — and what should already have happened before this point?
**Reflection:** Where in the TORGY's inbound track could you have first seen this coming — before it became a live descent decision?

## 2. Scan Discipline — the foundational throughline

Scan runs through all seven sessions, and it does **not** improve in a straight line.

- **Session 1** — "still some work needs to be done… scanning M98 properly." Arrivals ride to the end of their routes with no further instruction, get left high, need spins.
- **Session 2** — "still want to work more on the scan, but the fundamentals are starting to build."
- **Session 3** — real, plainly-logged improvement: "scan is starting to come together a bit more."
- **Session 5** — a regression. "Scan was the big issue tonight" — aircraft leveling off unflashed, or through 15–16,000 with no handoff started.
- **Session 6** — "scan and data entry are still the big issues," and this session's second loss of separation is tied to it directly: a BAINY descending through an overflight's altitude, not seen until too late.
- **Session 7** — the only unambiguous improvement callout in the whole record: "some good improvement on those fronts."

Session 7 followed session 6 faster than any other pair in the log — every other gap runs close to seven weeks. One data point is not proof, but it is the only short-interval pair on record, and it is also the only session with a clean positive note.

**Coaching Framework — guideline §13, Scan Discipline:** Scan should continuously answer four questions: which aircraft need action now, which will need it next, which could become a problem if ignored, and which are safely progressing without you. Priority order: immediate conflicts, final, base, departures, satellites, feeder gates — regardless of where an aircraft sits on the scope.
> *Confidence:* the trainee's own guideline framework, not an FAA/LOA citation.

**Why it happens:** Scan is not about looking at everything — it is prediction, not observation. The BAINY-over-overflight case is the clean example: the conflict was visible well before GEP, but nothing registered as "this needs action" until it was already inside minimum separation.

**The fix, unchanged every time it appears:** start the scan at the airport being actively worked and move outward through the sequence, then repeat — continuously, not just when something interrupts.

**Drill situation:** Combined position, moderate traffic. A BAINY arrival is approaching the end of its published route. No further clearance issued. Mid-coordination on something else.
**Ask:** What should already have happened before this moment, and what do you do right now?
**Reflection:** What is the earliest point in this aircraft's arrival where "no further instruction yet" should have registered as a problem?

## 3. Tunnel Vision — a different mechanism than scan breakdown

One explicit citation, session 6 — worth keeping separate from scan discipline because the mechanism differs. Scan breakdown is *missing* something. Tunnel vision is *fixating* on one thing while missing everything else.

N442PL was a VFR bravo transition — a single aircraft needing to get squared away. While it was being worked, two other aircraft leveled off at 17,000 and called for higher. The note: "don't get tunnel visioned on trying to get VFR/popup IFR aircraft squared away in one fell swoop — keep working your traffic and fit in those aircraft every few transmissions instead."

Small sample — one citation — but a distinct failure mode, and exactly the kind of thing that gets lost if everything is filed under "scan."

**Coaching Framework — guideline §11 and §14, Workload Management:** Attention is a finite resource. Task fixation, tunnel vision and head-down time are listed as workload failure modes distinct from missed observation or late recognition — they need a different fix: reduce cognitive load and interleave tasks, not just "look around more."
> *Confidence:* the trainee's own guideline framework, not an FAA/LOA citation.

**Why it happens:** A single aircraft needing several steps to resolve — a popup, a bravo transition, a coordination call — pulls attention into a tunnel because it feels like it needs finishing before moving on. The rest of the traffic does not stop needing attention during that.

**The fix, as the instructor gave it:** do not try to finish the multi-step task in one pass. Work one piece, go back to the scan, come back to it. Every few transmissions, not every few minutes.

**Drill situation:** Working a VFR bravo transition through the airspace — identifying, squawking, sequencing. Two other aircraft also being worked level off at 17,000 and are due a climb.
**Ask:** How do you handle the bravo transition without losing the other two?
**Reflection:** What is the signal that tells you it is time to break off the multi-step task and go back to the scan?

## 4. Clearance Completeness — something assumed instead of stated

Different specific mistakes, same shape underneath.

- **Sessions 2 and 5** — turns to the final approach course without the explicit instruction to join it. Legal intercept, but the aircraft is never told to join, and in at least one case it goes through the localizer.
- **Session 5** — issuing a heading to a popup IFR aircraft without folding it into the clearance itself — saying "via radar vectors" instead of just stating the heading already being given.
- **Session 7** — N6398G, a popup IFR Skyhawk. Radar identified, given a heading and an altitude — but never given an actual IFR clearance until prompted. The fix: "cleared to STP airport via fly heading X, climb and maintain X," no gap where VFR-or-IFR is ambiguous.
- **Session 7** — before discontinuing vertical or lateral separation between two aircraft, both have to be cleared for the approach (or one visual, one instrument). SKW3905 and DAL261 were both still uncleared when vertical separation got discontinued.

None of these are the same mistake. They are the same failure to state something explicitly instead of assuming the aircraft — or the read — will fill the gap.

**Documented Requirement — FAA JO 7110.65 (exact paragraph not independently verified):** MSP's own order cites JO 7110.65 Ch. 7 directly for VFR clearance completeness (altitude restriction plus frequency, every time). The IFR-specific citation for "state every element, never imply one" was **not** independently pulled from the order text — FAA-grounded in principle, session-sourced in the specific phrasing the instructor gave.
> *Confidence:* medium. Confirm against 7110.65 before citing a specific paragraph to anyone else.

**Why it happens:** An instruction that is technically legal but not explicitly stated leaves the read up to the pilot — and VATSIM pilots, per the trainee's own notes, are already slow on frequency changes. "Cleared for the approach" and "cleared to join the localizer" are not the same clearance as a heading that happens to end up on the localizer.

**Pattern-level fix:** before keying up, ask whether what is about to be said fully resolves the aircraft's next action, or whether it relies on the pilot inferring it. If it relies on inference, add the sentence.

**Drill situation:** A Skyhawk calls up VFR, requesting a pop-up IFR to STP. Radar-identified, then issued a heading and an altitude to get established toward the airport.
**Ask:** What is still missing before this aircraft is legally on an IFR clearance?
**Reflection:** What is the one phrase that, said every single time, makes this gap impossible?

## 5. Shoreview MVA — same callsign, same mistake, four sessions apart

**Critical.** The one where the same aircraft makes the same mistake twice.

- **Session 1** — N167DP, an STP arrival, descended to 3,000 for a shallow ILS 14 intercept, drives into the Shoreview Antenna MVA north of STP.
- **Session 5** — N167DP again. Same airport, same approach, same altitude, same result: "drove through the Shoreview Antennas MVA… at 3,000' on their base turn."

Same callsign, same mistake, same fix given both times: keep them at or above 3,500 until established on the localizer, or turn them wider so the question does not come up. It has not reappeared in sessions 6 or 7 — but those sessions did not run the same STP scenario, so that is an **absence of data, not a confirmed fix**.

**Documented Requirement — M98 7110.26, Ch. 8-1(c), obstruction data:** Arden Hills/Shoreview Antennas charted at MSP bearing/distance 018°/11, height 2,438 ft — one of seven listed obstructions in the order. The 3,500 ft floor is the **instructor's stated operational guidance** for staying clear of it on the STP 14 base turn, not a number independently re-derived from the MVA chart (Appendix E is a chart image).
> *Confidence:* high on obstruction height and position; session-sourced, not independently chart-verified, on the 3,500 ft operational number.

**Why it happens:** A shallow intercept on ILS 14 puts the descent point close to the antenna before the aircraft is established on the localizer — where the MVA, not the glideslope, is what protects against the obstruction.

**The fix, both times:** do not descend STP 14 arrivals below 3,500 until established. If a shallower intercept is tempting for spacing, that is exactly the case where the MVA bites.

**Drill situation:** STP arrival for the ILS 14, shallow-intercept geometry. Tempting to descend early for spacing before the aircraft is established on the localizer.
**Ask:** What altitude do you assign, and why — and what is actually protecting the aircraft from the Shoreview obstruction at that point in the approach?
**Reflection:** Session 1 to session 5 is four sessions and the same mistake. What would need to change about how this gets practiced for it not to make it to a ninth session?

## 6. Phraseology — minor individually, persistent as a pattern

Two of these corrections are **word-for-word identical between session 6 and session 7**, which is the clean evidence they have not stuck yet.

- **Session 2** — "zero zero" instead of "double-o" in altimeters; say "point" in frequencies.
- **Session 4** — "Minneapolis Approach," not "Minnie," on initial contact.
- **Session 6** — "zero" instead of "oh" in numbers; "cleared visual approach, runway X," not "cleared visual, runway X."
- **Session 7** — the exact same two corrections, verbatim. Plus one new one: shortened N-callsigns need the aircraft type or "November" — never just the bare digits.

Tactical, not strategic — quick to fix, easy to drill. The value is repetition until automatic, not a debrief.

| Said | Should be | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "Cleared visual, runway one two right" | "Cleared visual **approach**, runway one two right" | Repeated verbatim in sessions 6 and 7 — say "approach." |
| "…zero, **oh**, five, **oh**" | "…zero, **zero**, five, **zero**" | Say "zero," never "oh" — also repeated verbatim S6→S7. |
| "Altimeter two niner **double-o**" | "Altimeter two niner **zero zero**" | "Double-o" invites mishearing — spell out both zeros. |
| "Contact departure, one two zero **eight**" | "Contact departure, one two zero **point** eight" | Omitting "point" risks the pilot hearing a different frequency entirely. |
| "**Minnie**, N7DP, with you" | "**Minneapolis Approach**, N7DP, with you" | Full facility name on initial contact — session 4. |
| "…**7DP**, contact departure" | "…**Premier 7DP**, contact departure" (or "N7DP") | Bare digits after shortening drop the aircraft type or "November" — session 7, new this time. |

Voicing rules for callsigns, flight numbers and runway designators are in `claude_US_Carrier_Callsigns.md` and `claude_SID_TTS_Voicing.md`.
